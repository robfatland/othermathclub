# Semi-Baked Visualization Ideas

A collection of mathematical curiosities suitable for animated visualization.
Some are well-formed; some need further thought.

---

## 1. Force-Directed Planar Graph Relaxation

Start with a planar graph (> 20 vertices, reasonably dense mean degree).
Pin four edges to the corners of a square. Render the initial layout with
many crossing edges (random vertex placement). Model all edges as springs
(Hooke's law) with repulsive forces between non-adjacent vertices
(Coulomb-like). Animate the relaxation: the graph untangles itself into a
crossing-free planar embedding.

The visual payoff is watching the mess of crossings resolve itself into
clean planarity — a force-directed layout that converges to a proof of
planarity.

**Notes:**
- Need to ensure the chosen graph is actually planar (e.g., a triangulation,
  or a maximal planar graph on 25+ vertices).
- Tufte-Fruchterman-Reingold forces work well here.
- Pinning 4 vertices to a square boundary helps constrain the outer face.
- Could show edge-crossing count as an overlay that ticks down to zero.

## 2. Double Hypercube (Shared Cells)

Consider a cube as one of the 8 cells of a 4D hypercube. Can that same cube
also be a cell of a *different* 4D hypercube?

**Maximum shared cells between two distinct tesseracts:**

Two distinct tesseracts (in general position in 4D) can share at most
**1 cubic cell**. Here's why: a tesseract is determined by a vertex and 4
mutually orthogonal edge directions. If two tesseracts share a cubic cell,
they share 3 edge directions and differ in the 4th. The cube lies in a
3D hyperplane; one tesseract extends in one direction from that hyperplane,
the other in a different direction (or the opposite direction). They cannot
share a second cell because their 4th-dimension extensions diverge — any
other cell of tesseract A uses the 4th direction that tesseract B doesn't
have, and vice versa.

Special case: if two tesseracts share a cubic cell and extend in *opposite*
directions along the same 4th axis, they share exactly 1 cell and together
form a shape like two tesseracts glued along a cube (a "dipyramid" analog).

**Visualization idea:** Render two tesseracts that share one cubic face,
projected to 3D. Color the shared cube distinctly. Rotate in 4D to show
the relationship. The two-tesseract complex has 2×16 - 8 = 24 vertices
(sharing the 8 vertices of the common cube), and 2×32 - 12 = 52 edges.
Manageable wireframe density.

## 3. Sierpiński Tetrahedron: Four Faces, Four Methods

Render a 3D tetrahedron where each of the four faces displays the Sierpiński
gasket, but each face is generated by a *different* method:

### Face 1: Chaos Game
Pick a random point inside the triangle. Repeatedly choose a random vertex
and move halfway toward it. Plot each point. After ~100,000 iterations the
gasket emerges.

### Face 2: Pascal's Triangle Parity
Compute Pascal's triangle mod 2 down to row 1025. Odd entries get a dot;
even entries are blank. The resulting pattern is the Sierpiński gasket.
Map the triangular grid of dots onto the triangular face.

### Face 3: L-System Curve
Axiom: `X`
Rules: `X → Y+X+Y`, `Y → X-Y-X`
After n iterations, traverse the resulting string:
- `X`, `Y`: ignored (no drawing)
- `+`: draw a short segment, then turn right 60°
- `-`: draw a short segment, then turn left 60°

This traces the Sierpiński arrowhead curve, which fills the gasket.

### Face 4: Midpoint Subdivision
Start with a triangle. Connect the three edge midpoints, creating 4 sub-
triangles. Discard the central triangle. Recurse on the 3 remaining
peripheral triangles. After ~8-10 iterations, the gasket is resolved.

**Animation:** Rotate the tetrahedron slowly so all four faces are visible
in sequence (or simultaneously from a well-chosen angle). Could also show
each face "building" via its respective method during the first few seconds
before assembling into the solid tetrahedron.

## 4. Hopf Fibration

The 3-sphere S³ can be decomposed into a continuous family of circles
(fibers), each linked with every other. Project a subset of these fibers
from 4D to 3D via stereographic projection. The result looks like nested,
interlocking tori. Animate by:
- Sweeping through fibers (parameter controls which latitude circle on S²
  maps to which torus)
- Rotating the entire fibration in 4D
- Color-coding fibers by their base point on S²

This is arguably the most beautiful structure in topology.

## 5. Modular Multiplication Circles (Times Tables)

Place n points equally spaced on a circle. For each point k, draw a line
from k to (k × m) mod n. Animate m continuously from 2 to n. At integer
values of m you get the classic "times table" patterns; between integers
the lines interpolate smoothly. Cardioids, nephroids, and complex star
patterns emerge and dissolve.

Simple to implement, hypnotic to watch.

## 6. Mandelbrot Zoom

Continuous zoom into a region of the Mandelbrot set (e.g., the seahorse
valley or a Misiurewicz point). Color by smooth iteration count with a
palette that cycles. Render at high resolution with deep iteration limits
as the zoom progresses (iteration count must increase with magnification).

Technically straightforward but computationally intensive. Could use
perturbation theory for deep zooms.

---

## 7. Lorenz System: Sensitivity to Initial Conditions

Two Lorenz trajectories starting from nearly identical initial conditions
(differ by 10⁻¹⁰ in one coordinate). They track together for a while,
then diverge explosively. Animate both simultaneously, color-coded, with
a running display of their separation distance. The visual: two dots that
move in lockstep, then suddenly split and wander independently on the
attractor.

---

## 8. Geodesics on Curved Surfaces

Animate particles following geodesics on interesting surfaces:
- Torus (geodesics that wrap around both directions)
- Klein bottle (non-orientable, geodesic returns mirror-flipped)
- Hyperbolic plane (Poincaré disk model, geodesics as circular arcs)

The Poincaré disk is particularly visual: "straight lines" that appear
as arcs, with the metric distortion making things near the boundary appear
to slow down.

---

## 9. Fourier Epicycles

Decompose any closed 2D curve (e.g., a hand-drawn outline) into a sum of
rotating circles (Fourier series in the complex plane). Animate the circles
spinning, with the tip of the last circle tracing out the original curve.
Start with few circles (rough approximation) and progressively add more
(convergence to the target).

---

## 10. Penrose Tiling Growth

Animate the construction of a Penrose tiling via inflation/deflation.
Start with a single tile, subdivide, assemble, repeat. The aperiodic
structure grows outward. Color tiles by type (kite/dart or thick/thin
rhombus). Alternatively: use de Bruijn's pentagrid method and animate
by sweeping the grid offsets.

---

## 11. Strange Attractors Gallery

Beyond Lorenz: animate several strange attractors side by side or in
sequence:
- Rössler (single-lobe spiral with fold)
- Chen (double-scroll)
- Halvorsen (three-fold symmetric)
- Thomas (cylinder-like with slow spiral decay)
- Aizawa (torus breaking into chaos)

Each with a trail that builds up over time, colored by speed or curvature.

## 12. Sphere Eversion (Conceptual)

Turning a sphere inside out without tearing or creasing (but allowing
self-intersection). The Thurston corrugation method or the Morin surface
halfway point. Extremely ambitious to render properly but visually iconic.

## 13. Interlocking Möbius Strips

Consider a Möbius strip shaped as an oblate loop (like a racetrack oval)
where the 180° twist is concentrated in the two U-bends at opposite ends.
This leaves two relatively flat "nice square" regions in the straightaways,
each roughly the width of the strip in side length, where the surface
locally looks like an ordinary rectangle — one displaced slightly above
the other.

**Two-strip arrangement (plus-sign):** Take two such Möbius strips and
arrange them so the nice squares of one are glued flush to the nice squares
of the other, with their respective longitudinal axes at 90°. The overall
shape is a 3D plus-sign. An ant walking longitudinally on strip A
encounters four opportunities to switch to strip B: two transitions at
each of the two shared squares (entering and exiting each square).

**Three-strip arrangement (triangle):** Extend to three strips at 120°
separation in a triangular arrangement, each pair sharing glued nice-square
regions.

**Questions:**

- Are the resulting constructions topologically interesting, or merely
  unremarkable extensions of a single Möbius strip?
- What surface (or surface complex) results from the gluing? Is the
  composite orientable or non-orientable? What is its Euler characteristic?
- How many distinct boundary curves does the composite have?
- Does the three-strip version produce anything qualitatively different
  from the two-strip version?

*__AI Response:__ This is genuinely interesting — the gluing changes
everything compared to merely linking two strips. When you glue the nice
squares face-to-face, you're performing a topological identification: two
regions of strip A are identified with two regions of strip B. The result
is no longer two separate surfaces but a single 2-complex (a surface with
branch lines along the gluing boundaries).*

*Consider what the ant experiences. Starting on strip A, walking
longitudinally, it hits the first shared square. Here the surface is
locally doubled — two sheets glued together. The ant can continue on A
or switch to B. If it switches to B, it's now traveling at 90° to its
original direction. After traversing B's half-loop (through one twisted
U-bend), it arrives at the second shared square, where it can switch back
to A — but now it's on the "other side" of A due to B's half-twist.*

*The key question is whether the identification produces a manifold or a
non-manifold complex. At the interior of each shared square, the surface
has two sheets glued together — this is fine (it's just a double covering
locally). But at the boundaries of the shared squares, the sheets
separate, which creates branch curves. The resulting space is a
**branched surface** — not a manifold, but a 2-complex with a well-defined
topology.*

*For the two-strip version: each strip contributes one boundary curve
(since each is Möbius), but the gluing doesn't close off the boundaries.
The composite has 2 boundary curves. Its Euler characteristic depends on
how we count: if we treat the shared squares as identified (not just
stacked), we're essentially taking two Möbius strips and identifying two
disk-shaped regions of each, which gives χ = 2(χ_Möbius) - 2(χ_disk) =
2(0) - 2(1) = -2. This is the same as a genus-2 non-orientable surface
with boundary — a Klein bottle with holes, essentially.*

*The three-strip version is qualitatively richer: the ant now has
branching choices at 6 junction squares (each strip shares two squares
with each of the other two strips). The resulting complex has the flavor
of a non-orientable surface with a trivalent graph of branch curves — a
structure studied in the theory of train tracks and branched surfaces in
3-manifold topology. It is definitely not a trivial extension.*

*For visualization: render the strips as semi-transparent surfaces, color-
code each strip, and trace an ant's path as it navigates the junctions.
The visual payoff is showing that a creature confined to this surface
can reach "everywhere" despite the apparently separate-looking loops.*
